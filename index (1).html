1Hemodynamics: The Big Idea

CO = SV × HR SV = EDV − ESV EF = SV ÷ EDV × 100 MAP = DBP + (SBP − DBP)/3 BP ≈ CO × SVR

Stroke volume depends on three things

  • Preload: how full and stretched the ventricle is at end-diastole. More stretch gives a stronger contraction (Frank-Starling), up to a limit.
  • Afterload: the resistance the ventricle pushes against to eject (HTN, vasoconstriction, aortic stenosis). ↑ Afterload = ↑ workload and O₂ demand.
  • Contractility: the squeeze force, independent of stretch. SNS and positive inotropes ↑ it; MI and cardiomyopathy ↓ it.
Preload and afterload are NOT opposites. They're independent. A hypertensive patient in fluid overload has ↑ preload AND ↑ afterload.
The HR trap: CO = SV × HR, but a very fast HR (e.g., 180) shortens diastole, so filling and SV fall and CO decreases. Coronary arteries fill in diastole, so tachycardia also cuts O₂ supply.
Normal values to know: CO ≈ 4–8 L/min · EF 50–70% · MAP ≥ 65 mmHg for organ perfusion · BNP < 100 pg/mL · NT-proBNP < 300 pg/mL.

Classify by the HIGHER category. For example, 142/78 is Stage 2 because the SBP alone qualifies. HTN is diagnosed over a series of visits or with continuous monitoring, never from one reading. Pain and anxiety activate the SNS.

Risk factors: non-modifiable

  • Age
  • Family history
  • Race (higher in Black adults)
  • Sex

Risk factors: modifiable (teaching targets)

  • Smoking
  • High-sodium diet
  • Obesity and physical inactivity
  • Excess alcohol, stress
  • Hyperlipidemia, diabetes

Three physiologic systems drive HTN

  • RAAS: ↓ renal perfusion → renin → angiotensin II (vasoconstriction, ↑ SVR) + aldosterone (Na⁺ and H₂O retention, ↑ volume).
  • SNS: ↑ HR (chronotropic) + ↑ contractility (inotropic) = ↑ CO; vasoconstriction = ↑ SVR.
  • Endothelial dysfunction: ↓ nitric oxide → impaired vasodilation, inflammation, plaque formation.

Vascular damage chain

Sustained ↑ BP→Shearing force on endothelium→Chronic inflammation→Arteriosclerosis (hardening) + Atherosclerosis (plaque)

Cigarette smoke

  • ↑ SNS response: ↑ SBP and DBP, ↑ HR, ↑ CO and coronary flow demand, so more O₂ demand.
  • Pro-thrombotic: ↑ platelet aggregation, ↑ platelet adhesiveness, ↑ blood viscosity, ↓ clotting time, so clots form on plaques (MI, stroke).

4Coronary Artery Disease, ACS & MI

Supply vs. Demand: the framework for every chest-pain question

↓ Supply (coronary blood flow)
  • Vasospasm (e.g., cocaine)
  • Fixed stenosis (plaque)
  • Thrombosis (plaque rupture)
  • Tachycardia (less diastolic filling)
↑ Demand (O₂ consumption)
  • ↑ Heart rate
  • ↑ Contractility
  • ↑ Afterload
  • ↑ Preload
  • (Fever, exertion, LVH)
Atherosclerotic plaque→Plaque rupture→Thrombus→Ischemia (reversible)→↓ ATP→Necrosis = MI (irreversible)→Troponin released

Classic MI

  • Sudden, severe, persistent chest pain
  • Heavy, crushing, tightness
  • Radiates to L arm or shoulder, jaw, neck, back
  • Not relieved by rest (vs stable angina)

Atypical MI (~1 in 5!)

  • Fatigue, dyspnea
  • Nausea, "indigestion," epigastric pain
  • Diaphoresis, dizziness
  • Jaw or back discomfort only
  • Women, older adults, diabetics
Clinical judgment: Any suspected MI means a 12-lead ECG first. Never dismiss atypical symptoms in a high-risk patient as "just GI" or "just tired."

MI diagnosis: 3 findings

  1. Clinical presentation
  2. 12-lead ECG
  3. Cardiac enzymes (troponin)

6Valve Disorders & Murmurs

Stenosis: "not enough blood passes through"

  • Valve doesn't open fully, so the chamber behind it works harder (pressure overload, hypertrophy)
  • Aortic and mitral are most common
  • Causes: rheumatic fever scarring, wear and tear (elderly), congenital

Regurgitation (incompetence): "blood leaks backwards"

  • Valve doesn't close, so the chamber gets volume overload and dilates
  • Causes: congenital or prolapse, rheumatic fever, cardiomyopathy, endocarditis, MI (papillary muscle)
Systolic murmursDiastolic murmurs
Aortic stenosis · Pulmonic stenosis
Mitral regurgitation · Tricuspid regurgitation		Aortic regurgitation · Pulmonic regurgitation
Mitral stenosis · Tricuspid stenosis
Logic, not memorization: In systole the semilunar valves (aortic, pulmonic) should be OPEN and the AV valves (mitral, tricuspid) CLOSED. So semilunar stenosis or AV regurgitation causes a systolic murmur. Flip everything for diastole.

What would these patients present with?

  • Aortic stenosis: exertional syncope, angina, dyspnea (fixed outflow, CO can't rise, LVH raises O₂ demand)
  • Mitral stenosis: LA enlargement → atrial fibrillation, pulmonary congestion, dyspnea
  • Mitral regurgitation: backflow to LA and lungs → dyspnea, crackles. Acute post-MI MR causes sudden pulmonary edema.
  • Aortic regurgitation: LV volume overload → dilation → HF
  • Tricuspid regurgitation: backflow to RA → JVD, peripheral edema

7Heart Failure

Definition: a complex clinical syndrome with subjective symptoms (dyspnea, fatigue) and objective findings (edema, crackles), caused by any disorder that impairs the ventricle's ability to fill or eject.

Compensation: helpful at first, harmful later

MechanismShort-term benefitLong-term harm
SNS activation↑ HR, ↑ contractility → ↑ CO↑ O₂ demand, vasoconstriction (↑ afterload), dysrhythmias
Na⁺ and fluid retention (RAAS)↑ volume → ↑ preload → ↑ SV (Frank-Starling)Congestion: pulmonary and peripheral edema
DilationMore stretch → stronger contractionOverstretched fibers → weaker contraction → ↓ CO
HypertrophyMore muscle → more forceImpaired filling, ↑ O₂ needs, poor coronary flow, ventricular dysrhythmias

Decompensated HF / pulmonary edema red flags: worsening dyspnea and orthopnea, new crackles, pink frothy sputum, ↓ SpO₂, rapid weight gain (≈1 kg per day = 1 L fluid), ↑ JVD, cool skin, confusion. Priority: high Fowler's, O₂, notify the provider.

Natriuretic peptides

  • ANP from the atria; BNP from the ventricles. Both are released with stretch and promote Na⁺ and H₂O excretion and vasodilation.
  • Normal: BNP < 100 pg/mL, NT-proBNP < 300 pg/mL (age-dependent).
  • Higher BNP means more stretch and worse prognosis. Falling = improving. Rising = worsening, even before symptoms.

EF math: EDV 120 mL, ESV 84 mL → SV 36 → EF 30% → HFrEF. Normal EF ≈ 50–70%. A normal EF does NOT rule out heart failure.

9Lecture Case: Mrs. Smith (worked through)

A 72-year-old woman with HTN, hyperlipidemia, and an MI 10 years ago (2 stents). She has had a year of increasing exertional dyspnea, sleeps on several pillows, and noticed foot swelling 2 weeks ago. Exam: tachypnea, bibasilar crackles, cool skin, 2+ pitting pedal edema. CXR: lower-lung fluid, LV and RV hypertrophy.

Step 1: Recognize cues
  • Risk history: HTN (↑ afterload), hyperlipidemia (atherosclerosis), prior MI (lost myocardium)
  • Left-sided cues: exertional dyspnea, orthopnea (pillows), tachypnea, crackles, fluid on CXR
  • Right-sided cues: new pitting pedal edema
  • Low output cue: cool skin (SNS vasoconstriction)
  • Remodeling: biventricular hypertrophy
Step 2: Explain the pathophysiology from 10 years ago to today
HTN + hyperlipidemia→Atherosclerotic CAD→MI (10 yrs ago)→Necrosis → scar→↓ Contractility, ↓ SV→Compensation: SNS, RAAS, remodeling→Hypertrophy + dilation; chronic ↑ afterload from HTN→Compensation fails → LEFT HF (dyspnea, orthopnea, crackles)→↑ Pulmonary pressures → RV afterload ↑→RIGHT HF (pedal edema)

Today she has biventricular HF, likely HFrEF given the prior MI (expect an S3, EF < 40%). Long-standing HTN also contributes to stiffness.

Step 3: Prioritize, act, and evaluate
  • Priority problem: impaired gas exchange from pulmonary congestion (ABCs).
  • Expected additional findings: ↑ BNP, JVD, hepatomegaly, S3, weight gain.
  • Nursing actions: high Fowler's, O₂, monitor SpO₂, lung sounds, daily weights, I&O, and BNP trend. Notify for worsening.
  • Evaluate improvement: fewer crackles, sleeping with fewer pillows, weight down, BNP falling, SpO₂ up.

10Clinical Judgment Test Strategy

  • Recognize cues → analyze → prioritize → act → evaluate. For every finding, ask "what's the mechanism?" then "what does it threaten?"
  • Prioritize with ABCs and stability: airway and breathing (pulmonary edema, SpO₂ ↓) > circulation (MAP < 65, chest pain unrelieved by rest, new ST elevation) > stable chronic findings > teaching.
  • Unstable beats expected: new, sudden, or worsening findings beat chronic, unchanged ones.
  • Every compensation has a cost: tachycardia ↑ demand and ↓ filling, vasoconstriction ↑ afterload, fluid retention causes congestion.
  • Left = lungs, Right = rest of the body. Locate the backup to find the failing side.
  • Lab timing matters: early normal troponin ≠ no MI; CK for reinfarction; BNP trend for HF.
  • Atypical is still an MI. Women, older adults, diabetics: fatigue, nausea, dyspnea.
